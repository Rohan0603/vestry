## 1. Mission

A durable local control plane in which one developer states a goal once, and a deterministic Chief runs the entire software delivery lifecycle — discovery, architecture, planning, decomposition, implementation, independent review, verification with proofs, and closure — using Trello as its project-management record (D42, revision 1.8), spawning specialised agents through a supervised adapter contract, and continuing without manual follow-up until machine-checkable acceptance criteria pass and the Owner accepts.

The one-sentence form: **Vestry is an autonomous engineering organisation in a box, for one person, on one machine, that cannot lie to you about what it did.**

---

## 2. Why v2 exists — the shift from v1

v1 was a **supervisor**. A human queued tasks, external agents executed them, and the orchestrator policed dispatch, cancellation, evidence and recovery. It reached 75% of its Rust migration milestone and a **NO-GO** release status, with 10 issues still in progress and its final cutover qualification gate sitting unstarted in backlog.

v2 is a **self-running organisation**. The human supplies intent, not task management. The Chief owns product management and the lifecycle; supervision becomes the substrate that makes unattended autonomy safe enough to leave running, rather than the product itself.

Three v1 constraints are explicitly removed:

1. **Stack complexity.** Electron shell + TypeScript main + separate Rust core sidecar + C++ Win32 helper + `better-sqlite3` rebuilt for a specific Electron ABI. v2 is one Rust binary.
2. **Single-worker limit.** v0.1 ran one active run/worker by default. v2 runs N.
3. **Governance documented beyond its implementation.** v1's approval register described exact-action, expiring, single-use approvals in more detail than `evaluatePolicy` actually enforced. v2 puts the envelope in code and proves it with one conformance harness.

Two v1 constraints are **kept**, because they were correct:

- Worktrees isolate file changes and are **not** a security sandbox.
- A worker's success claim stops at verification. It never becomes acceptance on its own.

---

## 3. Users and operating context

**Primary user:** one developer, the Owner, who is simultaneously requester, approver and final reviewer. Not a team. Not a tenant. Not a service.

**Context:** Windows-first local desktop, loopback only, existing local Git repositories as the work source (not orchestrator-managed clones). Single-user assumption is what allows v2 to be local-only and authentication-free while remaining safe. **Scope note (D38, revision 1.4):** the authentication-free assumption no longer covers protected mutation and approval routes — those require an Owner session established through the OS-local single-use bootstrap decided in D38; loopback binding and Host/Origin checks alone never authenticate the Owner. The single-user, local-only character of the product is unchanged.

**Operating expectation:** the Owner starts a goal, walks away, and returns to either a finished result awaiting acceptance or a short, specific list of things that genuinely need a human.

### Change control (binding)

Any change to a D-numbered decision requires a **new** D-numbered entry stating what it supersedes and why. Decisions are never edited in place and never deleted. A superseded row keeps its number and gains a "superseded by D-nn" marker. The same rule applies to §17 risks and §18 open items: an item is closed by a recorded entry, not by removal.

This is not bureaucracy. v1 moved its adapter priority from ACP-first — approved in HOD-1, HOD-2, MVP-3 and Q1 — to server-plus-pinned-SDK in AC-TD-02 and ORC-22, with no superseding record anywhere. The resulting ambiguity is why ORC-54 and ORC-52 needed reconciliation, and why ORC-254 was filed as a blocker against approval documents that were in fact fully readable. Unrecorded drift is cheap to prevent and expensive to reverse.

## 5. Roles

**Owner (human).** States the goal. Sets the policy envelope. Is the escalation target. Accepts at the three gates and at goal completion.

**Chief (deterministic Rust core).** Engineering and product manager. Clarifies requirements, owns scope and roadmap, decomposes and sequences work, assigns agents, tracks progress on the bound Trello board (D42, revision 1.8), handles blockers, enforces the policy envelope, decides when acceptance criteria are met, and requests final acceptance. Owns all durable state. Is not an LLM; it *calls* LLM agents for planning and judgement, and it is the only component permitted to write authoritative state.

**Architect (LLM agent).** Produces HLD, then LLD, plus decision records with provenance.

**Planner (LLM agent, may be the same invocation as Architect).** Turns design into a dependency graph of sized, parallelisable issues with per-issue acceptance checks.

**Worker (LLM agent via adapter).** Implements exactly one task, in one leased worktree, under TDD discipline.

**Reviewer (LLM agent via adapter).** Reviews independently. Must never be the same agent instance, adapter process, or attempt that produced the work under review.

**Verifier (deterministic, may invoke an LLM for judgement on ambiguous output).** Runs builds, tests and acceptance checks; records commands, exit codes, output, and evidence hashes.

**Doctor (deterministic).** Reconciles after a crash or restart: orphaned processes, leased worktrees, unresolved attempts, unverified project-record writes on the bound Trello board (D42), dirty user changes.

**Messenger (deterministic).** Durable, ordered, idempotent message routing between agents via the transactional outbox and inbox. Not an LLM.

## 6. Lifecycle

### Phase 0 — Intake
Owner supplies the goal, target repository or repositories, constraints, and the policy envelope. Entry points: local UI, a card on the bound Trello board assigned or labelled for the Chief (D42), or the standing priority queue. All three feed the same durable SQLite queue, ordered by priority and dependency.

### Phase 1 — Discovery
Chief asks a **bounded** set of clarifying questions (bounded so an unattended run cannot stall on an infinite clarification loop), then produces a **requirement charter**: problem statement, scope, stakeholder, functional and non-functional requirements, constraints, **acceptance criteria expressed as executable checks**, and explicit non-goals. Approval of the charter freezes the immutable **goal anchor** (§6 cross-cutting, D33).
→ **Gate 1: Owner approves the charter.**

### Phase 2 — Architecture
**HLD:** components, boundaries, data model, technology choices, integration points, risks, and the trade-offs taken.
**LLD:** modules, interfaces, schemas, error handling, test strategy.
Each decision is recorded with provenance, distinguishing *proposed* from *approved*. Written to the bound Trello board (D42) and mirrored as markdown in the target repository.
→ **Gate 2: Owner approves HLD and LLD.**

### Phase 3 — Planning and decomposition
Milestones, epics, sized issues, dependency graph with cycle detection, parallelisation plan, per-issue acceptance checks, and per-issue token/time estimates (estimates are advisory — see D16). Written to the bound Trello board (D42). Each issue carries exactly one executor classification, exactly one task-type classification, component labels identifying affected subsystems, objectively measurable acceptance criteria, and its `blockedBy` dependencies (§10).
→ **Gate 3: Owner approves the plan.**

### Phase 3→4 — Readiness certification (D27)
Gate 3 approval does **not** authorise dispatch. Before any Worker is launched on an issue, the Chief certifies readiness against a fixed checklist and writes a dated certification to the issue:

1. One clearly defined outcome and purpose.
2. Scope and exclusions explicitly stated.
3. Applicable approved decisions and architecture contracts identified.
4. Every mandatory predecessor completed **and independently verified** — not merely closed.
5. Current repository branch, base SHA, and the relevant existing implementation inspected.
6. External dependencies, versions, source references and compatibility constraints known.
7. Steps specific enough for a Worker to execute without inventing scope.
8. Required interfaces, inputs, outputs, schemas and error behaviour defined.
9. Acceptance criteria objectively measurable.
10. Positive, negative, regression, cancellation and recovery test expectations identified.
11. Validation commands and environment specified.
12. Security, privacy, permissions, licensing and rollback implications addressed.
13. No unresolved architecture-changing decision hidden inside the coding scope.

The certification is a **machine-checked precondition, not a human gate** — the Chief writes it, deterministically, from durable state, so D8's "then free run" is unaffected. It is **invalidated** by any material change to requirements, dependencies, approved decisions, or the repository baseline SHA, and is never reused across such a change. A card carrying a dispatchable classification, sitting in a Backlog or Ready list, or free of obvious blockers is still not ready until certified. If a check cannot be satisfied the issue stays unready, the gap is opened as a linked issue, and the issue waits — a Worker is never dispatched onto an uncertified issue to "keep work moving".

### Phase 4 — Execution loop (autonomous)
For each unblocked, readiness-certified issue:
1. Lease a Git worktree, recording branch and base SHA.
2. Post a `START` marker (§10) with the approach, source references, dependency state, branch and base SHA, expected changes, tests and risks.
3. Dispatch a Worker as an independent OpenCode process inside the lease.
4. Worker implements under TDD (red → green → refactor → verify).
5. Verifier runs builds, tests and the issue's acceptance checks, recording every scenario as `PASS`, `FAIL` or `NOT RUN` with a reason (D29).
6. Reviewer independently reviews. Cannot be the authoring instance. The review **binds to the exact commit SHA reviewed** (D30).
7. Bounded repair — at most 2 cycles. Any commit produced during repair **invalidates the prior review**, which must be re-obtained against the new SHA; the risk tier is reassessed against the actual diff, and an upward reclassification re-routes the approval to the higher tier. On exhaustion the issue parks as a Tier-C attention item.
8. Proofs attach to the Trello card (D42): commands, exit codes, test output, diff summary, base SHA and reviewed SHA, worktree path, reviewer verdict, evidence hashes, confidence rating, and the recorded rollback path for the change.
9. Issue reaches `verified_handoff` on the bound Trello board (D42) with proofs attached; the execution lease retires only after all owned processes are independently gone and every change is safely recorded, with source and evidence retained; successors may proceed from the independently verified immutable candidate. **Closure and merged-SHA CLOSEOUT occur only after the Phase 5 merge is observed (D36, revision 1.3; supersedes the former close-before-integration wording).**

No acceptance criterion, non-functional target or test expectation may be reduced, relaxed, reinterpreted or deleted in order to make step 5 or step 7 pass (D31). A criterion that cannot be met is a `FAIL` or a `NOT RUN` with a reason, and becomes an attention item.

### Phase 5 — Integration and closure
Cross-issue integration checks, full regression run, final evidence bundle for the goal, pull request opened per policy. After an Owner-authorized, independently reviewed merge is observed, each integrated issue is closed with its proofs and receives the merged-SHA `CLOSEOUT` (D36). **Completion of a parent is never inferred from completion of its children** — a milestone or epic is closed only when its own acceptance criteria are independently verified, which is a stronger statement than the union of its child issues passing.

### Phase 6 — Acceptance
Chief declares all acceptance checks passing, produces the evidence bundle, and raises a **goal acceptance** attention item. The bundle states which **anchor version** was satisfied and lists that anchor's criteria with their `PASS` / `FAIL` / `NOT RUN` outcomes and traceability edges — **adding a criterion at this point is prohibited**, since a goal is judged only against the anchor it started from. The Owner accepts or rejects with comments. Rejection re-enters the loop as new constraints against the same anchor, not as a fresh goal.

### Cross-cutting — Recovery and continuation
At any point, a crash or restart resumes from durable state. Unknown or disconnected work becomes `recovery_required` and is never silently replayed. Dirty user changes are preserved. A goal is resumed, never restarted.

### Cross-cutting — Goal anchor and drift control (D33)

Drift is the characteristic failure of an unattended loop: nothing crashes, every task passes, and the result is not what was asked for. The mitigation cannot be a periodic human re-approval — that reintroduces the manual follow-up the product exists to remove — and it cannot be an LLM asked "does this still look like the goal", which is unfalsifiable. It has to be a **graph property the Chief can compute.**

**The anchor.** At Gate 1, the goal statement plus every acceptance criterion are frozen into an immutable, hash-identified **goal anchor** in SQLite, with each criterion carrying a stable identifier. The anchor is referenced by ID from then on. Gate 1 approval is approval *of the anchor*, which is why the anchor's hash appears in the evidence bundle at Phase 6.

**Traceability, required on both edges.** Every plan node, Trello card (D42), lease, dispatch, verification run, review and merge must cite the anchor ID and at least one criterion ID it advances. A criterion may be advanced by several pieces of work; no piece of work may advance zero. **Scope (D37, revision 1.3):** dispatches necessarily preceding Gate 1 — discovery/charter proposal only — bind the Owner's durable intake/target/scratch scope instead of the not-yet-frozen anchor, with no implementation or merge authority; from the moment the anchor is frozen, every dispatch falls under this rule without exception.

**The two drift signals, computed before every anchored dispatch (D37), and again on the R9 audit cadence:**

- **Orphan work** — open issues or in-flight attempts tracing to no anchor criterion. This is scope accretion: the loop invented work nobody asked for. It is **not dispatched**; it is parked as an attention item, and the correct dispositions are only "it does serve a criterion, re-cite it" or "it is a genuinely new want, split it into a follow-up issue outside this goal".
- **Blind spots** — anchor criteria that no open work traces to and that are not yet `PASS`. This is silent abandonment: the loop has stopped trying. It is the more dangerous of the two, because the run looks healthy while the goal quietly goes unmet. It is raised immediately, and the Chief may only close it by adding work that cites the criterion.

**Re-derivation, not trust.** The Chief re-computes the traceability graph from durable state rather than trusting the graph it planned. A plan that drifted from its own recorded issues is itself a finding.

**Change is explicit or it did not happen.** Discovery that the goal was wrong produces a **charter amendment** — a new recorded decision, the Owner notified, the anchor re-frozen at a new version with the diff preserved. Amending an anchor is never done by editing it. Criteria may not be weakened (D31) and equally may not be silently grown; an amendment that only *adds* criteria is the accretion pattern this rule exists to catch, so the amendment record must state which criterion was wrong and why.

**Drift is bounded like everything else.** At most **two amendments per goal**. A third request means the goal is not understood, and the loop stops and returns it to the Owner to restate — the same shape as the repair-cap-then-park rule in §8, applied to intent rather than to code. An unbounded amendment count would make "the goal changed" indistinguishable from "we are lost".

**Rejection at acceptance is not a new goal.** It re-enters as recorded constraints against the existing anchor (Phase 6), so a rejected goal cannot be quietly re-chartered into something easier.

## 7. Autonomy envelope

**Tier A — fully autonomous.** Read anywhere the Owner has granted; edit inside a leased worktree; build; test; commit to a feature branch; spawn agents; write to the bound Trello board (D42); self-reschedule; retry a failed attempt within the retry cap; update issue status.

**Tier B — proceed and record**, only where the Owner's policy envelope pre-authorises the class of action. Push a non-protected feature branch; open a draft pull request; mark an issue done; cross a soft spend threshold. Every Tier-B action is recorded with the policy clause that authorised it.

**Tier C — always blocking.** Merge to main or master; force-push; anything touching a protected branch; credential use; network egress outside the allowlist; deleting data; spending real money; modifying the policy envelope itself; any agent editing the orchestrator; and any verification failure that has exhausted its repair budget.

**Mode semantics.** In **Default** mode, Tier B and Tier C require approval. In **YOLO** mode, application-level gates relax — but YOLO never overrides OS, Git-host, provider, credential, or Human-Only boundaries. **Hard-denied actions remain denied in every mode.** Approvals are exact-action (bound to the specific command and target), single-use, and expiring. An approval with a missing, non-numeric, or past expiry is a denial, not a grant.

**Fail-closed rule.** Missing, stale, ambiguous, or incomplete evidence is a failure. Absence of a denial is not a grant.

**Enforcement through the adapter (verified, D20 phases 2a and 2b).** The Tier model is encodable in OpenCode, but **not through CLI flags**. Headless `opencode run` has exactly two behaviours and neither is "ask a human": without `--auto` every permission request is **auto-rejected**; with `--auto` every request not explicitly denied is **approved once**. Escalation is therefore a Vestry-level loop — a rejected action fails the tool call, Vestry raises an attention item and re-dispatches with an amended policy.

**There is no safe default, and this reverses the phase-2a conclusion.** Phase 2a recorded that headless `ask` resolves to reject, "which is the safe direction." The mechanism is right and the consequence was wrong: the live probe found that OpenCode's built-in default ruleset at `packages/opencode/src/agent/agent.ts:119-136` is **`"*": "allow"`**, merged into every agent before user config. Under default-allow almost nothing ever raises a permission request, so there is no safe direction to fall back on. Confirmed empirically: `opencode run` with no `--auto` and no `OPENCODE_PERMISSION` executed `echo …` outright. **Headless OpenCode on a clean install runs arbitrary shell commands, edits files and spawns subagents with no approval of any kind.** This is the origin of D26.

Some protection is nonetheless free and Vestry inherits it rather than weakening it: `read` gates `*.env` and `*.env.*` behind `ask` (which headless resolves to reject) while allowing `*.env.example`, and `external_directory` gates writes outside the project directory.

Three enforcement surfaces exist, in order of preference:

1. **`OPENCODE_PERMISSION`** — an inline JSON permission policy deep-merged into config (`packages/opencode/src/config/config.ts:559-563`). Supports per-tool pattern rules, for example `bash: {"git *": "allow", "*": "ask"}`. Preferred: distinct per attempt, nothing written into the leased worktree, no agent definition to manage, and **proven by the live probe to override the Owner's global config**.
2. **A per-dispatch agent definition** carrying a V2 `permissions` ruleset of `{ action, resource, effect: allow | deny | ask }`. An unresolved agent fails closed to deny-all.
3. **Config files** via `OPENCODE_CONFIG_CONTENT` / `OPENCODE_CONFIG_DIR`, also without touching the worktree. Note that both are **merge** operations on top of global config, not replacements for it (§15).

**Ordering rule (measured, phase 2b).** Within one tool's policy object, **the last matching key wins**, consistent with `propertyOrder: "original"` in the V1 permission schema. Two probes carrying the same two rules in opposite order behaved oppositely: `{"bash":{"*":"deny","echo *":"allow"}}` allowed the command; `{"bash":{"echo *":"allow","*":"deny"}}` removed the tool entirely. An allow-list is therefore expressible in exactly one order — wildcard first, specifics after:

```json
{"bash": {"*": "ask", "git status": "allow", "npm test": "allow", "cargo test": "allow"}}
```

Written the other way round it silently denies everything. The adapter must serialise policy objects in this order deterministically, and the conformance harness must carry a dedicated test for the reversed-order case, because the failure is silent and presents as a model refusing to cooperate.

**`deny` and `ask` are observably different, and `ask` is Vestry's default.** `deny` removes the tool from the model's tool list entirely; the model then hallucinates alternative names (it tried `shell` and `bash` when `bash` had been denied) and emits an `invalid` tool event, which `stats` counts. `ask` keeps the tool exposed and produces a **named, auditable rejection carrying the exact command**: `permission requested: bash (echo VESTRY_F_OK); auto-rejecting`. Vestry needs that string in the audit record — it is the difference between "the worker was denied X" and "the worker never had X". So: `ask` as the wildcard floor, `deny` reserved for actions that must never even be offered.

**Invalid JSON fails open, silently.** `OPENCODE_PERMISSION='{invalid json'` returned exit 0 with no warning on stdout or stderr and the command ran. The parse error is logged via `Effect.logWarning` at a level that never reaches CLI output, the merge is skipped, and default-allow stays in force. Combined with the finding above this is the worst failure mode in the whole verification: **a malformed policy string degrades to allow-everything, invisibly.** Vestry cannot treat "I set `OPENCODE_PERMISSION`" as "the policy is in force". Under D26 the adapter must (a) validate and serialise the JSON itself before spawning, and (b) run a **policy canary** — a dispatch under a deny-all policy asserting the tool is absent from the model's tool list — before any real work on a worker instance, installation or version.

Two hard rules follow. First, `--auto` and its two **hidden** aliases `--yolo` and `--dangerously-skip-permissions` all collapse into one boolean that disables the entire Tier model; passing any of the three spellings must be a recorded governed action requiring explicit YOLO authorisation, and the adapter must match all three. Second, OpenCode persists approved permissions per project and replays them as `allow` in every later session, so a single `"always"` reply permanently widens that project's envelope; Vestry must never emit an `"always"` reply, and must isolate or reset the saved-permission store per task.

**Binding layer (verified, D20 phase 2a/2c).** OpenCode 1.19.3 ships two parallel internal stacks — a live V1 stack under `packages/opencode/src/` and a partially ported V2 core under `packages/core/src/` — with **no switch between them** and the V2 core mounted only for debug and file/pty handlers. The V2 core's own TODO block lists unported Windows PowerShell and cmd handling, unported tree-sitter approval reduction and command-prefix arity, no `task` subagent tool, and undefined background-job persistence and restart recovery: precisely the areas §11 and §19 items 4–5 depend on. The adapter must therefore bind to the **external contract only** — CLI flags, HTTP/SSE routes, and the config and environment surface — and never to `packages/core` or `packages/opencode` internals. Then upstream completing its V1→V2 migration is a non-event. Evidence: `evidence/2026-11-06-opencode-1.19.3-v1-v2-stacks.md`.

## 8. Liveness requirements

Unattended operation is the feature; therefore the following are requirements, not tuning parameters.

- **Bounded loops.** Repair cycles ≤ 2 per issue. Retries ≤ 2 per attempt before the attempt becomes `recovery_required`. Clarifying questions in discovery are bounded.
- **Progress watchdog.** No durable state change for 10 minutes triggers diagnosis: is the process alive? does the adapter respond? Then one retry, then park with an attention item and continue with unblocked work. A parked issue never blocks the goal unless the dependency graph says it must. **Known collision to design around (D20 phase 2a, not contradicted by 2b):** OpenCode's bash tool defaults to a 2-minute timeout and caps at exactly 10 minutes (`MAX_TIMEOUT_MS`), and a long-running command emits no events during the call — the probe confirmed events arrive per *step* (`step_start`, `step_finish`), not within one. A legitimate 9-minute build therefore looks identical to a hung worker under the D15 window. The watchdog's progress signal must include process liveness and output activity, not only durable state change, or the timeout must be shortened below the watchdog window.
- **No spinning.** On exhaustion of any budget the Chief parks and escalates. It does not retry indefinitely and does not silently substitute a different provider, model, or route to get past a failure.
- **Cycle detection** at planning time, so the dependency graph cannot deadlock the scheduler.
- **Spend is observed, never enforced** (D16). Token and wall-clock consumption are tracked per attempt, per issue, and per goal, surfaced in the UI, and raise warnings at Owner-configured thresholds. **Work is never stopped for spend, and there is no circuit breaker.** This depends on the adapter surfacing per-call model, token counts and duration; an adapter that cannot report usage cannot support spend warnings at all, so that capability is a required part of the adapter contract (§18 item 1) and not an implementation detail to be discovered later. **Verified, D20 phase 2b — retraction of the phase-2a constraint.** Phase 2a concluded that live spend warnings could not be built on `run`'s stdout because usage lives on the assistant message and never surfaces in the stream. That is retracted. `opencode run --format json` carries usage on every **`step_finish`** part: `tokens.{total,input,output,reasoning,cache.read,cache.write}` and `cost`, arriving as the run proceeds. Live spend warnings under D16 are therefore buildable on `run` alone, and the choice between `run --format json` and a per-worker `serve` is **no longer forced by the usage question** (§11, §18 item 1). Three parser requirements fall out: the outer `type` uses underscores (`step_finish`) while `part.type` uses hyphens (`step-finish`) and a parser must not conflate them; each part carries a `snapshot` SHA that Vestry should record as a free provenance handle; and `opencode stats` is aggregate-only (no per-session breakdown, no `--session` filter) so it can cross-check but cannot drive live warning. **Cost floor, measured:** ~23,926 input tokens for a one-word reply — the system prompt plus tool schemas — with `cache.read` of 21,920 against `input` of 2,018 on later steps in the same session, so caching does engage. Any Vestry cost model starts from ~20–24K input tokens per call, not from the size of the task prompt. On free-tier routing `cost` reports `$0.00`, so the probe says nothing about cost *accuracy*; milestone 1 re-measures on a pinned model.
- **Idempotent resume.** After a crash the Chief reconstructs in-flight state from SQLite and reconciles with the OS and Git before dispatching anything new.

## 9. Termination — how the Chief knows the goal is achieved

The goal terminates when **all** of the following hold:

1. Every acceptance criterion recorded in the charter has an executable check, and every check passes under the Verifier, recorded as `PASS` — never blank, never omitted (D29). A check that could not be executed is `NOT RUN` with a reason and **fails condition 1**; it does not silently drop out of the count.
2. Every issue in the plan is closed with proofs attached. Completion of a parent is never inferred from completion of its children.
3. The Reviewer has signed off on the integrated result, bound to the exact SHA reviewed (D30).
4. An evidence bundle exists for the goal, with confidence ratings, exact commands and revisions, and stated limitations and threats to validity.
5. **The Owner has accepted.**

Chief self-assessment is never sufficient. A green build is never sufficient. If acceptance criteria cannot be expressed as executable checks during discovery, that limitation is stated in the charter at Gate 1 rather than discovered at final acceptance.

**Termination cannot be reached by weakening the goal.** No acceptance criterion, non-functional target or test expectation may be reduced, relaxed, reinterpreted or deleted in order to satisfy conditions 1–4 (D31). If the goal as chartered cannot be met, the correct outcome is a recorded `FAIL` or `NOT RUN` and an attention item to the Owner — not a quieter goal.

**Termination cannot be reached by drifting either (D33).** The bundle declares the anchor version it satisfies, and a goal is judged **only** against the criteria in that anchor. There must be **no open orphan work and no unresolved blind spot** when acceptance is raised — a criterion no work traces to is a `FAIL`, never an omission from the list, and work that traces to no criterion may not be presented as though it did. An anchor amendment may not be raised in the same breath as acceptance: if the delivered result only satisfies an easier goal, the honest record is that this goal failed.

## 10. Project-management integration contract (Trello, D42)

> **Superseded carrier — revision 1.8 (D42).** Until 12 November 2026 this section specified a Linear integration contract. D42 removes Linear and GitHub Projects as the system of record for both repository task work and the product's project-management integration, and makes Trello the sole system of record. The Linear-specific text below is retained as the historical record of the pre-cutover contract and is no longer a requirement. The structural requirements it carried remain binding with Trello as the carrier: (a) the authority split — the project record is authoritative for scope and intent and SQLite stays authoritative for runtime state; (b) mandatory read-back before any project-record write is reported as succeeded; (c) capability probing with limitations surfaced honestly; (d) credentials never enter the project record; (e) the D28 typed-marker vocabulary on every status transition, written to both the project record and SQLite and parsed deterministically, never as free text; (f) the issue/card taxonomy with exactly one executor and one task-type classification, and the rule that a classification does not authorise a start; (g) the discrepancy rule and one-authoritative-source-per-concern rule. The Trello-specific mapping — board and list structure, card fields, comment serialisation, idempotency keys and the `trello_card_id ↔ task_id ↔ attempt_id` binding — is design work carried in the revision-6 HLD/LLD and is **pending fresh Owner Gate-2 approval** (D30, D41); no Trello protocol is claimed implemented.

**Dual source of truth, split explicitly:**

- **Linear is authoritative for scope and intent** — projects, milestones, epics, issues, acceptance criteria, statuses, comments, design documents, decision records, evidence links. It is what the Owner reads.
- **SQLite is authoritative for runtime state** — goals, tasks, runs, attempts, leases, dispatches, approvals, policy evaluations, worktree and base-SHA bindings, evidence hashes, recovery state, outbox and inbox, spend. It is what survives a kill.

**Binding.** A mapping table binds `linear_issue_id ↔ task_id ↔ attempt_id`. Every Linear write carries an idempotency key so a post-crash retry cannot duplicate issues or comments.

**Read-back rule.** No Linear write is reported as succeeded without reading it back. "I called the API" is not evidence of synchronisation.

**Capability probing.** Connector capabilities are probed at startup and limitations surfaced honestly in the UI. v1 lessons: the connector cannot create Kanban views (ORC-55 never completed), and ORC-254 blocked real reconciliation work on a stale claim about document truncation that turned out to be false. An autonomous Chief that assumes connector capabilities will silently stall or fabricate progress.

**Probe result, 6 November 2026 — the connector cannot create teams.** Enumerating all 78 tools exposed by the Linear connector found `list_teams` and `get_team` but no `save_team` or `create_team`, while `save_project` (create/update, `setTeams`, `leadTeam`), `save_issue`, `save_document` (including reparenting to a team), `save_milestone`, `save_issue_label`, `save_project_label`, `save_initiative`, `save_release` and `save_status_update` all exist. Team creation is therefore an **Owner-only action performed in the Linear UI**, and any Vestry workflow that needs a new team must stop and raise an attention item rather than assume it. Confirmed live: the workspace has exactly one team, Mission Control (`ORC`, `8ac0d7aa-fbea-4f6a-9c56-5610bb8c015f`), and the Vestry project P-ORC-9 currently sits inside it — so Vestry issues inherit v1's `ORC-###` identifier namespace until the Owner creates a dedicated team and the project is repointed. This is the same class of gap as ORC-55, found by probing rather than by failing.

**Resolution, 6 November 2026.** The Owner created the Vestry team (`VES`, `701f2be8-7e3b-4f20-9363-f5112194f787`) in the Linear UI, and the project was repointed to it via `save_project` with `setTeams` and `leadTeam`, removing Mission Control. Read back and verified. Three things the probe did not predict, now recorded: **(a)** the repoint **changed the project identifier** from `P-ORC-9` to `P-VES-9`, because a project's identifier is derived from its lead team's key — so repointing a project with existing references is a breaking rename even though the UUID is stable; **(b)** the project status survived, and re-resolving `state: "backlog"` under the new lead team returned the *same* status ID `64fa6d8e-b9b1-47a8-92f8-1dc3494b2217`, so project statuses are workspace-scoped in this workspace and the feared `state` invalidation did not occur; **(c)** `get_team` returns no project-status or workflow inventory, so the connector cannot enumerate a team's valid project states before writing one — an agent must write and read back rather than pre-validate. Vestry issues will now carry the `VES-###` namespace. The underlying gap stands: **team creation remains Owner-only and unreachable from the connector**, and any Vestry workflow needing a new team must stop and raise an attention item.

**Write scope.** The Chief may create and update milestones, epics, issues, statuses, comments, documents and evidence links **within its bound project only**. It may not touch other teams or projects, may not delete history, and may not rewrite issues the Owner authored.

**Secrets.** Credentials and sensitive payloads never enter Linear — not in issue bodies, comments, documents, or attachments.

**Comment protocol (D28).** A fixed vocabulary of typed markers is the machine-readable channel between Vestry and Linear: `START`, `PROGRESS`, `BLOCKED`, `HANDOFF`, `REVIEW HANDOFF`, `CLOSEOUT`. Each transition writes the corresponding marker with its required fields — `START` carries approach, source references, dependency state, branch and base SHA, expected changes, tests and risks; `BLOCKED` names the blocking item, its owner, and the **precise unblock condition**; `REVIEW HANDOFF` carries the acceptance checklist, exact test results, PR and SHA, limitations, security impact, rollback path and required reviewer action; `CLOSEOUT` carries the merged SHA, the criteria completed, and remaining linked follow-ups. The deterministic Chief parses these markers and never free text. **Every status transition writes to Linear *and* to SQLite** — progress recorded only in SQLite is invisible to the Owner, and progress recorded only in Linear does not survive a kill.

**Issue taxonomy.** Every actionable issue carries exactly one executor classification, exactly one task-type classification, component identifiers identifying the affected subsystems, objectively measurable acceptance criteria, and its dependencies. **An executor classification does not authorise starting the issue**, and neither does a Trello list position (Backlog or Ready) or the absence of an obvious blocker — authorisation comes only from the D27 readiness certification.

**Discrepancy rule.** When Linear and SQLite disagree, or when two governance documents disagree, Vestry follows the source that is authoritative for that concern (§10's split above), **records the discrepancy as an attention item, and never assumes reconciliation happened silently.** Daemon Reborn's own §15 documents this failure mode against itself — its `AGENTS.md` still carries superseded role labels months after the approved workflow changed — and v1 hit it with ORC-254, which blocked real reconciliation work on a stale claim. An unattended loop that quietly picks one of two conflicting sources will diverge from both.

**One authoritative source per concern, never a third.** Linear holds scope and intent; SQLite holds runtime state. Vestry must not grow a third store that also claims authority over either concern — not a markdown backlog, not a JSON state file, not a cache treated as truth.

## 11. Supervision requirements

This is the capability v1 never proved, and it drives v2's design outward from the supervision layer.

- **Provable termination.** The orchestrator must demonstrate that a cancelled worker and **all its descendants** are gone. An abort call returning success is not termination. ("Abort ACK ≠ descendant termination.") **Verified finding (D20 phases 2a and 2b):** OpenCode 1.19.3 does implement a real tree kill on the live path — `/session/:id/abort` → `Fiber.interrupt` → `AbortSignal` → `ShellTool`'s `raceAll([exitCode, abort, timeout])` → `handle.kill({ forceKillAfter: "3 seconds" })` → `taskkill /pid N /T /F` on Windows or `process.kill(-pid, SIGTERM→SIGKILL)` on POSIX, piped through `Effect.orDie` so a failed kill propagates loudly. Background jobs are cancelled by transitive closure over session and parent-session, so subagent sessions are reached. That is materially better than v1 assumed and does **not** relax this requirement, for two reasons: the whole chain is **cooperative**, so a hard kill of OpenCode itself — exactly what the D15 watchdog and the process-console kill button do — runs no finalizer, no `orDie` and no `taskkill`, and orphans everything; and on Windows an already-exited parent gets no cleanup from the spawner's scope finalizer, orphaning detached grandchildren. **The orphan claim is no longer argued — it is empirically proven, twice, on the machine Vestry will run on (D20 phase 2b).** The observed tree was `timeout.exe → sh.exe (npm shim) → opencode.exe → bash.exe → sleep.exe`; killing only `opencode.exe` with `Stop-Process -Force` left `opencode.exe` and `bash.exe` gone and `sleep.exe` **alive** under a `ParentProcessId` pointing at a process that no longer existed, with four minutes of its own runtime left. Reproduced independently in a second mid-turn kill. Note also that `killTree` in `packages/core/src/shell.ts`, which does the right thing on both platforms, has **zero callers** at this tag and is dead code — an easy thing to mistake for the operative mechanism. Upstream carries an explicit unverified-coverage TODO at `packages/core/src/tool/bash.ts:75`. Vestry must therefore verify termination independently by enumerating its own Job Object, before and after, and never accept an adapter's abort result as proof. Two design consequences the probe added: the Win32 Job Object is not a refinement but **the** mechanism, since killing the worker process is insufficient by construction and the watchdog's kill path will orphan whatever the worker had spawned; and `opencode.exe` is a **native binary**, not a Node script, with two extra npm-shim process levels above it, so "find the node process and kill it" is not a strategy that would have worked here either.
- **Ownership.** Each worker process is created inside a Win32 Job Object (Windows) or a dedicated process group (Unix), behind one portable contract. Job ownership is provable; session ownership inside a shared server is not — this is the decisive reason for D5.
- **One process per worker.** Independent OpenCode processes, so a runaway or crashed worker cannot affect another, and so kill semantics are exact. D5 rejects *one shared server with N sessions*; it does not reject *N private servers, one per worker*. **The `run` versus `serve` choice is no longer forced (D20 phase 2b).** Phase 2a had concluded that usage and permission events arrive only on the server event stream, which would have pushed the adapter into per-worker `serve` despite the loopback HTTP exposure that brings. The probe disproved both halves: per-step `tokens` and `cost` arrive on `run --format json`'s stdout (§8), and an `ask` rejection prints a named, auditable line — `permission requested: bash (<cmd>); auto-rejecting` — on the same stream (§7). `run` is therefore sufficient for spend observation, permission signalling and audit capture, and is now the **default** transport, with `serve` retained only where a capability genuinely needs it (cooperative abort via `POST /session/:id/abort`, which is the one path phase 2b did not exercise). If per-worker `serve` is used anywhere, an ephemeral port discovered from child output, an explicitly pinned loopback hostname, and a Vestry-generated per-worker server password injected only into that worker's environment are all mandatory; `--mdns` remains hard-denied because it rebinds the hostname to `0.0.0.0`.
- **Isolation.** Each attempt gets its own worktree lease with a recorded base SHA and fencing. Worktrees are not a sandbox.
- **No collateral damage.** The orchestrator never terminates processes it does not own. Unrelated user processes, and the user's own dirty changes, are preserved.

## 12. Durable state and project memory

- SQLite in WAL mode, **one logical writer**, all state transitions transactional.
- **Transactional outbox and inbox** for every external effect (Trello writes, agent messages; D42), with sequence checking and idempotency, so effects are exactly-once in practice and never lost across a crash.
- Schema changes only via versioned migrations, applied before any component opens the database.
- **The audit record is mandatory and native.** Every governed action, policy evaluation, approval grant or denial, and state transition is persisted durably in SQLite with hashes, and is reachable through the evidence viewer and through the bound Trello board (D42). It is authoritative evidence, not observability: it must be locally verifiable, must survive a crash, and must never depend on an external backend being reachable or on an exporter's status code. Deferring a dedicated browsing surface for it (§13) is a UI decision only and does not weaken this requirement. **What it must additionally carry, after D20 phase 2b:** the permission policy as actually serialised (not as authored, since ordering is semantically load-bearing — §7), the policy-canary result for that worker instance, and the policy-relevant subset of the worker environment — PATH, shell resolution, and the loaded plugin list. A worker's behaviour is not reproducible from the prompt and the policy alone, because global plugins inject instructions Vestry does not compose (§15).
- **Adapter-side session state is a recoverable handle — verified, D20 phase 2b.** After a mid-turn hard kill of the worker, `opencode run --session <id>` returned coherent history: the interrupted turn's tool call had been persisted, the resumed session answered correctly about what it had run, and there was **no duplication and no silent replay** of the interrupted command. `opencode session list` works against a relocated store. This supports §8's idempotent-resume requirement and milestone item 5, within the limit of what one short turn proves — it does not establish behaviour when the killed turn was mid-write, mid-commit, or several tools deep. Two constraints follow: the session store belongs to the adapter, not to Vestry, so it can never be the authoritative record; and per-worker throwaway stores (`OPENCODE_DB`, §15) must be exported into the evidence bundle before deletion, or the handle is destroyed along with the data it points at.
- **Project memory lives outside any agent's context window.** Decisions, constraints, rejected approaches, learned facts, and the current understanding of the goal are persisted on the bound Trello board, in SQLite, and in repository markdown (D42), so a restarted Chief resumes with the same understanding instead of re-deriving it. A multi-hour autonomous run must outlive any context limit.
- **The goal anchor is durable state (D33).** The goal statement, the versioned criterion set with stable identifiers, the anchor hash, the traceability edges from every plan node and issue to the criteria it advances, the recorded charter amendments, and the computed orphan-work and blind-spot findings are all first-class SQLite records — not derived on demand from prose, and never reconstructed from Trello comment text (D42). Drift detection has to survive a kill and a restart, because the run that drifts is exactly the one nobody is watching.

## 13. Local web UI requirements

Loopback only (`127.0.0.1`), single user, no remote access. Eight surfaces are in scope for v2.0.

**In scope:**

1. **Goal list and live run timeline.** Every goal with its phase — discovery, architecture, planning, executing, blocked, awaiting acceptance — progress against the approved plan, and the live timeline of dispatches, reviews and verifications. The primary "what is happening" view. **Each goal also shows its anchor: the goal statement as accepted at Gate 1, the criterion list with per-criterion status, the anchor version and amendment count, and the traceability view of which open work cites which criterion.** The two drift signals are rendered as first-class state rather than derived off-screen — *orphan work* (activity citing no criterion) and *blind spots* (a criterion no open work cites) — because a goal quietly stopping being served is the failure this view exists to make visible while it is still cheap to correct.
2. **Attention inbox.** The aggregated human-action queue, modelled on the way Qoder surfaces "My attention": Tier-C approvals showing the exact action, target and expiry; parked blockers with diagnosis; goal-acceptance requests with the evidence bundle; spend warnings. Count badge, oldest first. This is the single place the Owner needs to look.
3. **Gate review workspace.** A dedicated screen for each of the three gates — charter, architecture, plan. Shows the artefact, what changed since the previous revision, and allows approve or return-with-comments. Comments re-enter the loop as constraints. This is where "no manual follow-ups" is actually earned.
4. **Evidence viewer.** The proofs behind any completion claim: commands run, exit codes, test output, file diffs, base SHA, worktree path, reviewer verdict, evidence hashes. This is what turns acceptance into a decision rather than a formality.
5. **Architecture and plan viewer.** Read the HLD and LLD, decision records with provenance, and the issue dependency graph. Lets the Owner judge whether a plan is coherent before execution rather than after.
6. **Agent and process console.** Per-worker live view: PID, adapter, command, streaming output tail, elapsed time, tokens so far, and an explicit kill button. The direct answer to "is it actually still running".
7. **Recovery and health dashboard.** The Doctor view: `recovery_required` items, orphaned processes, stale or unclaimed worktree leases, failed or unverified Trello writes (D42), migration state, restart history.
8. **Project-record sync, spend and policy editor (Trello, D42).** Two-way sync state — card mappings, last write, read-back confirmation, capability probe results, failed writes — plus token and time spend against warning thresholds, and the editable policy envelope: mode, allowlists, caps, and which Tier-B classes are pre-authorised. **Every control shown carries its enforcement status, `target` or `verified-enforced` (D32)**, visually distinguished, so the Owner can see at a glance which parts of the envelope are actually demonstrated executing rather than merely written down; a control may only be displayed as `verified-enforced` when a conformance-harness result exists for it. Also settings and bindings: Trello board binding (D42), adapter executable paths and versions, provider and model selection, notification preferences, and **secret references, never secret values**.

The eight surfaces are filtered, not reorganised, by six standing views the Owner can switch between — *needs my approval*, *blocked*, *in flight*, *awaiting review*, *awaiting verification*, *recently closed*. These are filters over the same underlying state, not queues that a human works through; Vestry dispatches automatically, and no view exists whose purpose is to make a person route work.

**Explicitly deferred out of v2.0 UI scope:** a dedicated worktree and repository browser; a dedicated audit trail and decision log screen; a goal replay and post-mortem screen. The audit *record* is unaffected by this and remains mandatory under §12.

## 14. Non-functional requirements

- **Local only.** Loopback binding, single user. No remote access, no multi-tenancy, no public-service behaviour.
- **One binary, no rebuilds.** No Electron, no C++ helper, no TypeScript backend, no native Node module compiled against a specific runtime ABI. Installation is copying an executable.
- **The worker runtime is a separate, external dependency.** The one-binary guarantee applies to Vestry, not to what it supervises. Verified on this machine 6 November 2026: target toolchain Rust 1.99.1 `stable-x86_64-pc-windows-msvc`; OpenCode 1.19.3 installed as an npm global under `%APPDATA%/npm/opencode`, so the worker plane requires Node 24.19.0 and `opencode` discoverable on PATH. The adapter contract must therefore treat runtime discovery, version reporting and version pinning as first-class capabilities, and must **fail closed** when the pinned version is absent or mismatched rather than silently proceeding against whatever happens to be installed.
- **Portable supervision.** Windows first, with the supervision contract portable to Unix process groups.
- **Truthful status over optimistic status.** The UI must never show a run as progressing when the orchestrator has lost track of it. Unknown is a state, and it is displayed.
- **Performance.** The daemon is idle-cheap; the UI is responsive while three workers stream output.
- **Recoverability.** Kill the daemon at any instant; on restart, no work is silently replayed, no lease is orphaned, no user change is lost.
- **Starts and is usable with nothing connected.** The daemon and the UI must start, and be fully usable for reading state, browsing history, viewing evidence and editing the policy envelope, with **no provider configured, no worker runtime present, no worker running, and no network.** A missing or mismatched OpenCode is a reported condition surfaced in the UI, not a startup failure and not a degraded guess. Nothing in the local experience may require an LLM call to render.
- **Reproducible rollback.** Every change the loop merges carries a recorded rollback path — the base SHA, the reviewed SHA, and the steps to return to base. A change without a rollback path is not mergeable.

## 15. Security and secrets

Secrets are injected **only** into the authorised worker process, at spawn time, and are never stored in prompts, SQLite rows, logs, artefacts, telemetry, Trello content, or the UI (D42). The UI holds secret *references* (which credential, from where) and never values. Credentials, database files, `.env*` files, and runtime artefacts are never committed. Network egress is allowlisted; anything outside it is Tier C.

Adapter-specific requirements, added by the D20 phase-2 source read and phase-2b live probe:

- **Loopback is not an authentication boundary.** A running `opencode serve` exposes `PUT`/`DELETE /auth/:providerID` (inject or remove provider credentials), `PATCH /global/config`, `/global/dispose` and `POST /global/upgrade` over HTTP. Any local process that can reach the port can call them, and OpenCode's own basic auth is off unless `OPENCODE_SERVER_PASSWORD` is set. If Vestry runs any server-mode worker, it must generate a distinct password per worker, inject it only into that worker's environment, pin `--hostname 127.0.0.1` explicitly, use an ephemeral port discovered from child output, and hard-deny `--mdns` (which rebinds to `0.0.0.0`).
- **Persisted approvals are a privilege-escalation path.** OpenCode stores approved permission rules per project and replays them as `allow` in every later session, so one `"always"` reply permanently widens that project's envelope across runs and across workers. Vestry must never emit an `"always"` reply, and must isolate or reset the saved-permission store per task so an approval granted in one attempt cannot silently authorise a later one. This is the same principle as §7's single-use, expiring approvals, applied to state the adapter owns rather than state Vestry owns.
- **The target repository's own agent config is untrusted input.** OpenCode reads `opencode.json` / `opencode.jsonc` from the project directory and merges it into the worker's configuration, including model, permissions and plugins. A repository can therefore widen the envelope of the worker dispatched into it — a direct R8 injection vector that does not need any prompt text at all. Vestry must set `OPENCODE_DISABLE_PROJECT_CONFIG` on every dispatch, or otherwise guarantee that repository-local config cannot alter model, permission or plugin resolution, and must treat its presence as untrusted content under D21.
- **No flag suppresses the Owner's global config or global plugins (D20 phase 2b — corrects the scope of the bullet above).** `OPENCODE_DISABLE_PROJECT_CONFIG` removes the `.opencode` directories walked up from the working directory, and is still worth setting, but it closes the *project*-driven escape and leaves the *global* one open. Tested directly: `OPENCODE_PURE=1` still ran bash with no prompt — the flag reaches only `plugin/tui/runtime.ts:1089`, suppressing plugin origins in the TUI, not headless config loading; `OPENCODE_CONFIG_DIR` pointed at an empty directory likewise still ran bash, because `config/paths.ts:39` treats it as **additive** to a search list that already begins with `Global.Path.config`; and `OPENCODE_CONFIG_CONTENT` **merges** — `config.ts:264` uses those flags only to decide whether to *seed* a default global config file, never whether to load one. **There is no supported way to give a worker a hermetic configuration.** Isolation therefore comes from three things that do work: `OPENCODE_PERMISSION` overriding the merged result (proven, §7), `OPENCODE_DB` isolating state, and Vestry recording what it could not suppress. Globally installed plugins inject instructions into every worker prompt — observed directly in the probe transcript, where the model reasoned about a "brainstorming skill" that no probe input mentioned, sourced from a global plugin. **Vestry composes the task prompt; it does not compose the whole prompt.** A worker's behaviour is consequently not reproducible from the prompt and the permission policy alone, so the audit record must carry the loaded plugin list alongside the rest of the environment (next bullet).
- **The worker environment is not hermetic, and PATH collision is a live failure mode (D20 phase 2b).** OpenCode's bash tool runs in Git Bash with PATH inherited wholesale. During the probe, `cmd /c ver` did not reach the Windows command interpreter: `which -a cmd` showed `/c/Users/<user>/AppData/Roaming/npm/cmd` resolving **before** `/c/WINDOWS/System32/cmd`, so an npm-installed shim shadowed it. The worker asked for one binary, ran another, and reported the shim's error as if it were the command's output — nothing in OpenCode flagged the substitution. A worker can therefore run something other than what it asked for and still produce a confident report. Vestry owns the worker environment, which means it must record the policy-relevant subset of it in the audit record — PATH, shell resolution, and the loaded plugin list — and should treat unexplained tool-output mismatch as an environment-suspicion signal rather than a task failure.
- **`export --sanitize` is a usable first pass, not a sufficient control (D20 phase 2b).** Measured: a planted marker appeared 32 times in a raw `opencode export` (16,448 bytes) and **0** times with `--sanitize` (7,814 bytes). Sanitisation emits typed, self-describing placeholders that preserve structure and joinability — `[redacted:session-directory:<sessionID>]`, `[redacted:session-title:<sessionID>]` — while retaining session id, slug, project id and agent name, so a sanitised export can still be joined to Vestry's own records. **The unverified part matters:** the marker was planted in the transcript and the title, not in a file path, a tool argument, an environment dump or a diff — and a diff or a bash argument is exactly where a real secret appears. Vestry must apply its own redaction on top before anything reaches the project record (Trello, D42), evidence bundles, or telemetry, per §15's opening rule.
- **Relocating the worker database works and is required for isolation (D20 phase 2b).** `OPENCODE_DB` accepts an absolute path and creates a fresh store there, leaving the Owner's real store untouched; `auth.json` is a **separate file** from the database, so a relocated store still authenticates. Two constraints: the value must be a **Windows** absolute path (a Git-Bash-style `/c/Users/...` satisfies Node's `isAbsolute` on win32 and is handed to SQLite as `C:\c\Users\...`), and a typo in the variable name fails silently into the real store — so the adapter must assert the store it opened is the one it asked for, not merely that it set the variable.
- **The worker environment is part of the policy surface.** Permissions, config, database location, plugin loading and auto-update are all controlled by environment variables (`OPENCODE_PERMISSION`, `OPENCODE_CONFIG_CONTENT`, `OPENCODE_DB`, `OPENCODE_PURE`, `OPENCODE_DISABLE_AUTOUPDATE`). Vestry owns that environment for every worker it spawns, must record the policy-relevant subset of it in the audit record, and must set `OPENCODE_DISABLE_AUTOUPDATE` so a worker cannot change its own binary version mid-run. Because `OPENCODE_PERMISSION` silently skips invalid JSON, the adapter must verify the policy took effect rather than assume it.

General permission-gateway requirements, adopted 6 November 2026 from the Daemon Reborn security policy:

- **Denied actions are first-class audit records.** A denial is written to the hash-chained audit record with the requesting agent, the action, the rule that denied it, the timestamp and the outcome — **redacted**, per §15's opening rule, so the record proves the denial without preserving any secret the request contained. A denial that leaves no trace is indistinguishable from an action that was never attempted.
- **Action requests are validated before they are evaluated.** Typed-schema validation and a size bound apply to every request a Worker or agent raises. A request that does not conform is rejected as malformed, not evaluated on its merits, and cannot reach the approval path at all. Untrusted content, prompt injection and a malicious local client must not be able to bypass authorisation by producing a request the gateway did not expect.
- **Revocation mid-run is a tested path, not an assumed one.** The Owner may withdraw a pre-authorisation or narrow the envelope while workers are in flight. Vestry must define and test what happens next: the withdrawal takes effect at the next policy evaluation, in-flight actions already granted are not retroactively re-judged, no new action in the withdrawn class is granted, and any Worker whose task depended on the withdrawn class parks with a `BLOCKED` marker naming the precise unblock condition. **Cancellation, timeouts and permission revocation must each have a test**, not a design paragraph.
- **Read, write, execute and send are distinct permissions.** A grant of one is never a grant of another, and each is separately scoped to approved targets — repositories, paths, branches, processes, and network destinations.

## 16. Non-goals and hard denials

No remote access. No multi-tenancy. No public-service behaviour. No auto-merge. No force-push. No protected-branch bypass. No silent substitution of provider, model, or route. No delegated or nested workers beyond the Chief's own spawning. No termination of unrelated processes. No treating a worktree as a security boundary. No hand-editing of generated artefacts. No committing of credentials, databases, or runtime output.

A review-only request does not authorise fixes. Approval of a target is not implementation or qualification of it. Passing fixture tests does not prove live-provider behaviour, abrupt-crash recovery, independent review, or release qualification.

| R5 | **Adapter dependence.** OpenCode's cancellation, restart and provenance behaviour is outside the project's control. **No longer hypothetical:** the D20 phase-2 source read found upstream had already shipped `1.19.5` against the installed `1.19.3`; found `POST /global/upgrade`, by which a running instance can change its own version; and found four explicit TODOs in `packages/core/src/tool/bash.ts` admitting that process-group cleanup, Windows shell handling, background-job persistence and restart recovery are unfinished. The residual risk is that all of this was measured once, on one machine, against a rotating free-tier model. | D4, D5, D20, D26 | Adapter capability manifest with `supported` / `unsupported` / `conditional` / `unverified` states; anything `unverified` is treated as unsupported until proven by the conformance harness. Adapter discovers, reports and pins versions and fails closed on mismatch (§14). Vestry never calls `/global/upgrade` and treats an unexpected version change as fail-closed. Termination is verified independently by Job Object enumeration, never taken from the adapter (§11). Permission policy is set explicitly and **proven in force by a canary** on every dispatch path (D26). Milestone 1 re-proves the load-bearing findings against a pinned paid model. |
| R6 | **Three concurrent workers on one machine** contend for CPU, disk and provider rate limits. | D14 | Conservative cap; watchdog parks rather than pushes; concurrency is a policy-envelope setting the Owner can lower to 1. |
| R7 | **Governance re-inflating.** v1 documented approvals far beyond what it enforced. | §7, §13, D32 | The envelope lives in code, is exercised by one conformance harness, and every Tier-B action records the clause that authorised it. A rule nobody can point at executing is not a rule. **D32 makes the gap visible rather than latent:** every control is recorded as `target` or `verified-enforced`, and the policy editor displays the distinction. Daemon Reborn, whose governance this project just adopted from, records the identical gap against its own branch protection — required controls that are documented but whose live enforcement is unverified — which is evidence the failure mode is not specific to v1. |
| R8 | **Prompt injection from untrusted content.** An autonomous Chief reads repository files, issue bodies, documents and agent output, then acts with full Tier A and pre-authorised Tier B permissions. Any of that text can carry instructions. v1 had no comparable exposure, because a human queued every task and watched every run. **The exposure is wider than originally recorded (D20 phase 2b).** Three further channels exist that need no repository content and no untrusted document at all: the target repository's own `opencode.json`, which merges into the worker's model, permission and plugin resolution; **globally installed OpenCode plugins, which inject instructions into every worker prompt** — observed directly in the probe transcript, where the model reasoned about a skill no probe input had mentioned; and PATH collisions, which let a worker execute a different binary than the one it named and report the result as if it had run (§15). Vestry composes the task prompt; it does not compose the whole prompt, and no flag suppresses the global sources. | D21, D26, §7, §15, §16 | Ingested text is data, never instruction. No agent may alter its own tier, the policy envelope, or any approval state because of something it read. Tier C remains human, so every irreversible action is still gated by a person who did not read the injection. `OPENCODE_DISABLE_PROJECT_CONFIG` on every dispatch closes the repository-driven channel; `OPENCODE_PERMISSION` overriding the merged config plus a policy canary (D26) closes the permission half of the global channel; the audit record carries PATH, shell resolution and the loaded plugin list so a worker's behaviour is reconstructable after the fact. **Residual exposure accepted knowingly:** Tier A and pre-authorised Tier B actions can still be steered by injected text, and plugin-injected instructions cannot be suppressed at all — only recorded. Revisit at HLD if a cheaper mitigation appears. |
| R9 | **Governance drift in an unattended loop.** In Daemon Reborn a human notices when the documented workflow and the executed workflow diverge — its own §15 is proof, recording that `AGENTS.md` still carried superseded role labels after the approved workflow had moved on. Vestry has no such human in the room during a free-run phase. Drift accumulates invisibly: an issue classification that stops matching what was dispatched, a readiness certification that outlived the baseline SHA it certified against, a dependency cycle introduced by a later planning pass, a Tier-B class exercised without a recorded authorising clause, an approval reused across commits. Each is small; none raises an error; together they are how a project ends up documenting governance it does not enforce. v1 arrived at exactly that state (R7) with a human supervising. | D27, D30, D31, D32, §10 | A **recurring project-integrity audit** owned by the Chief and reported through the Doctor surface: issue coverage against the approved plan, classification consistency, dependency-cycle detection, stale or reused readiness certifications, approvals whose bound SHA has moved, architecture drift against the approved HLD/LLD, controls recorded `target` for longer than a configured interval, and Tier-B actions with no authorising clause. It runs on a fixed cadence during execution and at goal closure, writes findings as attention items rather than fixing them silently, and is itself recorded as `target` until a harness demonstrates it (D32). The audit is bounded like every other loop — it reports, it does not escalate indefinitely. **Residual risk accepted:** an audit written by the same project it audits can share its blind spots; independent review at final Owner acceptance remains the backstop. |
| R10 | **Goal drift — the loop stays healthy and stops serving the goal.** Every task passes, no crash occurs, spend is within bounds, and the delivered result is not what the Owner asked for. This is the characteristic failure mode of autonomy and the one this product's whole premise makes likely: with no human watching between Gate 3 and acceptance, there is no one to notice a plan quietly migrating toward easier work. Two directions, and the second is worse: **scope accretion** (the loop invents work beyond the goal, which is visible as activity) and **silent abandonment** (the loop stops attempting criteria it cannot satisfy, which looks like a healthy queue draining). D31 blocks weakening a criterion; it does not block quietly never meeting one. | D33, D31, §6 cross-cutting, R9 | The immutable **goal anchor** plus bidirectional traceability, computed as a graph property before every dispatch and again on the R9 audit cadence: **orphan work** (cites no criterion) is not dispatched and parks as an attention item; **blind spots** (a criterion no open work cites and that is not `PASS`) are raised immediately. Intent change is only ever an explicit, recorded, Owner-visible **charter amendment**, bounded at **two per goal** — a third returns the goal to the Owner rather than drifting further. Acceptance is judged only against the anchor version the goal started from, and a rejection re-enters as constraints, never as a fresh re-charter. **Residual risk:** the mechanism detects *disconnection* from the anchor, not an anchor that was itself a poor encoding of intent — that is R3's problem and Gate 1 remains the control for it. |

## 18. Still open — deferred to design, not to requirements

These are design questions, open at the Baseline 1.0 freeze by design. They did not block the freeze and are the architecture phase's input rather than a gap list. An item is closed by a recorded entry here, never by removal (§4 change control). Items 11–13 were added at revision 1.1 by the Daemon Reborn adoption.

1. **Adapter contract shape** — the exact `HarnessAdapter` surface, capability manifest schema, and how OpenCode's paths map onto independent-process supervision. Evidence: `evidence/2026-11-06-opencode-1.19.3-cli-surface.md` (phase 1), `evidence/2026-11-06-opencode-1.19.3-source-read.md` (phase 2a), `evidence/2026-11-06-opencode-1.19.3-v1-v2-stacks.md` (phase 2c, erratum against 2a) and `evidence/2026-11-06-opencode-1.19.3-live-probe.md` (phase 2b, which wins where it contradicts either source read).

   **Settled, and not to be re-litigated:**
   - *Binding layer*: the adapter binds to OpenCode's **external contract only** — CLI flags, HTTP/SSE routes, and the config and environment surface. It never binds to `packages/core` or `packages/opencode` internals. 1.19.3 ships two parallel internal stacks with no switch between them (live V1 under `packages/opencode/src/`, partially-ported V2 core under `packages/core/src/`), so binding to internals couples Vestry to a migration that is still in progress upstream. Binding to the external contract makes that migration completing a non-event, and leaves R5's pin-and-fail-closed as the only mitigation needed. Recorded in §7.
   - *Which stack fires, measured*: **V1.** The tool name cannot discriminate — both stacks expose `bash`, and upstream has deferred the rename to 2.0 (`tool/shell/id.ts` comment). The discriminator is the tool **list**: the live registry offers `edit, glob, grep, invalid, read, skill, task, todowrite, webfetch, write`, and `task` and `invalid` exist only in the V1 registry, which also lacks nothing that V2 has. `stats` independently counted `invalid` usage, so the V1 `InvalidTool` really is live. Correction to phase 2c: `ApplyPatchTool` is **not** V2-only — `packages/opencode/src/tool/registry.ts:31` imports it and line 115 instantiates it.
   - *Termination* is a fully traced cooperative chain ending in a real tree kill (`taskkill /T /F` on Windows, process-group signals on POSIX), with background jobs cancelled by transitive closure. On the live V1 path the kill is piped through `Effect.orDie`, so a failed kill propagates loudly rather than silently. It nonetheless skips cleanup for already-exited Windows parents, and **a hard kill of the worker orphans its descendants — proven twice on this machine**, with `sleep.exe` left alive under a dead parent (§11). Recorded in §11.
   - *Per-call usage* **is available on the default transport.** `run --format json` carries `tokens.{total,input,output,reasoning,cache.read,cache.write}` and `cost` on every `step_finish` part, plus a `snapshot` SHA usable as a provenance handle. This **retracts** the phase-2a conclusion that usage is absent from `run`'s stdout, and with it the claim that live spend warnings require server mode. `opencode stats` remains aggregate-only. Recorded in §8.
   - *Transport choice*: `run --format json` is the **default**, not a compromise. With usage and named `ask` rejections both on stdout, per-worker `serve` is needed only for capabilities `run` does not expose — principally cooperative abort via `POST /session/:id/abort`. The forced choice phase 2a created is withdrawn. Recorded in §11.
   - *Permission policy* is enforceable without `--auto`, through the **external** surface — `OPENCODE_PERMISSION` (inline JSON deep-merged into config, per-tool pattern rules, distinct per attempt, nothing written into the worktree) preferred, an agent `permissions` ruleset second, config files third — not through CLI flags. **Confirmed live to override the Owner's global config.** `--auto`/`--yolo`/`--dangerously-skip-permissions` are one boolean that disables the Tier model. Saved approvals replay as `allow` per project. Recorded in §7 and §15.
   - *Ordering rule*: within one tool's policy object the **last matching key wins**, so an allow-list must be serialised wildcard-first, specifics-after; reversed, it silently denies everything. Recorded in §7.
   - *`deny` versus `ask`*: `deny` removes the tool from the model's list, causing hallucinated tool names and counted `invalid` events; `ask` keeps it and emits an auditable `permission requested: bash (<cmd>); auto-rejecting`. `ask` is Vestry's wildcard floor. Recorded in §7.
   - *No safe default, and no hermetic config*: the built-in default is `"*": "allow"`, malformed policy JSON fails open silently, and **no flag suppresses global config or global plugins** (`OPENCODE_PURE` reaches only TUI plugin origins; `OPENCODE_CONFIG_DIR` is additive; `OPENCODE_CONFIG_CONTENT` merges). Hence D26. Recorded in §7 and §15.
   - *Environment contract, first draft*: `OPENCODE_DB` works with a Windows absolute path, gives each worker a throwaway store, and leaves `auth.json` (a separate file) authenticating normally. Measured growth is ~55 KB per session and ~18 KB per message. Recorded in §15 and item 10.
   - *Session resume reconciles*: after a mid-turn hard kill, `--session <id>` returned coherent history with no duplication and no silent replay of the interrupted command. `opencode session list` works against a relocated store. Recorded in §12's favour and milestone item 5's.
   - *Sanitised export*: `export --sanitize` reduced a planted marker from 32 occurrences to 0 with structure-preserving typed placeholders, retaining session id, slug, project id and agent name. Adequate as a **first pass** only; redaction of file paths, tool arguments, environment dumps and diffs is unverified. Recorded in §15.
   - *Server endpoints* v1 recorded are confirmed present at 1.19.3 (`/global/health`, `/global/event`), alongside `/event` (instance SSE), `PATCH /global/config`, `/global/dispose`, `POST /global/upgrade` and `PUT`/`DELETE /auth/:providerID`. Recorded in §11.

   **Recorded gaps, carried rather than blocking the freeze.** P2a (cooperative abort via `POST /session/:id/abort`) and P6 (live server endpoint probing) were not performed, because the endpoint paths were not re-derived during the probe. Neither can change a requirement: endpoint existence is settled from source at this tag, and Vestry verifies termination by Job Object enumeration regardless of what the cooperative path does. Both are re-tested in milestone 1 if the design ends up depending on cooperative abort.

   **P2a/P6 disposition, 7 November 2026 (source-pinned; no live probes):** P2a cooperative abort and P6 server probing are not required by the default CLI/NDJSON adapter contract. P2a is only an optional hint; completion depends on independent OS process-tree proof (VES-25/26). P6 endpoints are source-enumerated but not live qualified. A future server-backed profile must independently qualify its required routes before use. The originally recorded `NOT RUN` entries remain historically true. No product-runtime permission or termination control is thereby verified-enforced. *Additive closure entry under §4 change control (closed by a recorded entry, never by removal), taking §19 item 9's "explicitly re-recorded as capabilities the design does not depend on" alternative; derivation, negative decision and NOT RUN reasons in `evidence/2026-11-07-opencode-v1.19.3-p2a-p6-disposition.md` (VES-24).*

   **Still open for design:**
   - The contract must require per-call usage reporting, must declare how termination is **independently verified** rather than reported, and — if any server mode is used — must require the adapter to **discover the child's bound port from its output** rather than assume one, since OpenCode defaults `--port` to `0`.
   - The **policy canary** required by D26: its exact assertion (tool absent from the model's tool list under a deny-all policy), when it runs (per worker instance, per installation, per version, or per dispatch), and what fails closed when it does not pass.
   - Whether the TypeScript SDK, `packages/sdk-next`, or raw HTTP/SSE is the right client surface for the `serve`-only capabilities, and whether `opencode acp` (still alive at 1.19.3, so v1's ACP-first → server drift was a choice, not a necessity) has any role.
   - The full **environment contract** the adapter must set on every dispatch, and which subset is policy-relevant enough to belong in the audit record (§15). Known members at 1.19.3: `OPENCODE_PERMISSION`, `OPENCODE_DISABLE_PROJECT_CONFIG`, `OPENCODE_CONFIG_CONTENT`, `OPENCODE_CONFIG_DIR`, `OPENCODE_DB`, `OPENCODE_DISABLE_AUTOUPDATE`, `OPENCODE_PURE`, `OPENCODE_GIT_BASH_PATH`, `OPENCODE_EXPERIMENTAL_WORKSPACES`, `OPENCODE_SERVER_PASSWORD`, `OPENCODE_SERVER_USERNAME`, `OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_ENDPOINT`, `OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_HEADERS`.
   - Version pinning and fail-closed behaviour on mismatch: installed `1.19.3`, upstream already at `1.19.5`, and `POST /global/upgrade` lets an instance change its own version at runtime. Vestry must never call it and must treat an unexpected version change as fail-closed (§14, R5).
2. **How the Chief calls LLM agents for planning** — same adapter contract as workers, or a lighter-weight non-leased invocation path with no worktree and no repo mutation.
3. **Schema** for goals, tasks, runs, attempts, leases, approvals, evidence, outbox, inbox, memory and project-record mappings (Trello cards — D42; the earlier Linear mapping name is superseded in scope only).
4. **Acceptance-check expression format** — how a criterion becomes an executable check, and what happens to criteria that resist mechanisation.
5. **Warning-threshold defaults** for spend, given there are no hard caps.
6. **Bounded clarification** — the exact question budget in discovery and what the Chief does when it is exhausted with the requirement still ambiguous.
7. **UI technology** — served static bundle versus embedded assets; no framework decision is implied by this document.
   - **Delegated selection recorded (D34/D40, revision 1.6; Gate-2 draft, not a baseline requirement).** The HLD/LLD revision-5 drafts select a build-time browser bundle — React 19 + TypeScript + Vite, Tailwind 4 with selected shadcn/Radix primitives, TanStack Query/Virtual — embedded into the single Rust executable through a compile-time `rust-embed` asset manifest (debug builds may serve from a filesystem directory; release builds embed). Rejected alternatives: a loose required UI directory or runtime filesystem lookup (breaks copy-one-executable installation), a runtime Node/TypeScript backend (breaks D3), CDN fonts/scripts or remote assets and any LLM call to render (breaks §14 offline/loopback), server-side rendering (unnecessary for a local single-Owner SPA), and a second client-side state authority (the UI renders daemon-supplied authority and never owns it). TanStack Virtual is recorded as not React-exclusive. This is a reversible delegated selection inside the approved envelope; the item stays open and the Owner ratifies or rejects it at Gate 2. No bundle size, accessibility or streaming performance is claimed measured.
8. **Provider and model policy** — what the Owner may pin, and how pinning is enforced without silent substitution.
9. **Audit and telemetry tooling** — deferred by decision D19 on 6 November 2026 until item 1 is settled, because what can be exported depends on what an adapter can observe. The following is already researched and is not to be re-litigated:

   - **LangChain is rejected.** Wrong layer and wrong language: it is a Python/JS LLM orchestration framework, so adopting it breaks D3 (Rust only, one binary) and D7 (the deterministic Chief owns state, scheduling and effects). It would compete with the Chief for control of the loop, and framework-mediated orchestration is what makes crash recovery unprovable.
   - **LangSmith is ruled out in all three deployment forms.** Cloud processes prompts and completions in third-party US or EU regions, against §14 (local-only, loopback-only) and §15 (payloads never leave the machine). Self-hosted requires an Enterprise plan and a license key. BYOC is AWS-hosted.
   - **Leading candidate: native OpenTelemetry from Rust.** `opentelemetry` 0.33.1 (319M downloads), `opentelemetry_sdk`, `opentelemetry-otlp` (189M) and `tracing-opentelemetry` 0.34.0, all updated October 2026 — mature enough that D3 survives intact. Causal spans along goal → issue → attempt → lease → dispatch → tool call → verification → review, using GenAI semantic conventions for model, tokens and duration. Prompt and completion *contents* stay out of spans; §15 applies to telemetry exactly as it applies to logs. Export OTLP to loopback only, optional and pluggable, defaulting to no backend. Self-hosted Langfuse (MIT core, OTLP endpoint at `/api/public/otel/v1/traces`, air-gap capable) or Jaeger/Tempo as Owner-installed extras, never required.
   - **Invariant regardless of choice:** the audit record stays native and authoritative under §12. OTLP export is fire-and-forget by design and a 200 response is not proof of persistence — Langfuse's own self-hosting tracker carries an open bug where OTLP ingestion returns 200 and the data never lands in ClickHouse. The same read-back discipline §10 applies to the project record (D42) applies here.
   - **Cost of the Langfuse option, stated honestly:** self-hosting v4 means Docker Compose with ClickHouse, Redis, Postgres, a web container and a worker — a multi-service stack beside a daemon whose installation requirement is "copy one executable." Hence optional, Owner-installed, and Vestry must be fully functional with nothing attached.
   - **v1's ORC-49** (OpenTelemetry-compatible causal run tracing) is promoted from backlog aspiration to built-in.
   - **Narrowed by D20 phase 2a — OpenCode already exports OTLP natively.** `packages/core/src/observability/otlp.ts` reads `Flag.OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_ENDPOINT` and `Flag.OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_HEADERS`, dynamically imports `@opentelemetry/exporter-trace-otlp-http` and constructs an `OTLPTraceExporter`. So worker-side traces can reach the same loopback collector as Vestry's own Rust spans **without any adapter-side instrumentation**, by setting two environment variables — which also makes the env surface (§7 binding layer) part of the telemetry contract, not just the policy contract. This does not settle the backend choice and does not weaken the invariant above: the adapter-side export is fire-and-forget from a process Vestry does not own, so its absence is not evidence of absence and its 200 is not proof of persistence. Vestry's own audit record stays the authoritative trace.
   - **Precondition status after D20 phase 2b.** D19 deferred this item until item 1 was settled, because what can be exported depends on what an adapter can observe. The observation question is now settled in the affirmative and by two independent routes: per-step `tokens` and `cost` arrive on `run --format json`'s stdout, and worker-side OTLP export is available for two environment variables. What remains is a design choice (backend, or no backend), not a research gap. The deferral stands until architecture picks it up.
10. **Non-functional targets** — §14 is deliberately qualitative at revision 0.9 (D25). Architecture must propose concrete numbers for: idle and peak memory with three concurrent workers plus the daemon; cold-start time to first dispatch; UI responsiveness while three workers stream output; and database growth and retention over long autonomous runs. Propose them against a real design rather than inheriting guesses from v1. **Measured, D20 phase 2b — adapter-side store growth is now a known quantity and a solved problem.** The local OpenCode session store at `%USERPROFILE%/.local/share/opencode/opencode.db` is **2.25 GB** with an 11 MB WAL after roughly a month of interactive use by one person; the probe measured its own relocated store at **983,040 bytes for 18 sessions and 54 messages**, i.e. roughly **55 KB per session and 18 KB per message**, which puts the Owner's 2.25 GB at on the order of 40,000 sessions. Extrapolated to three concurrent autonomous workers running unattended, that is real disk pressure — but `OPENCODE_DB` accepts an absolute path and gives each worker a throwaway store (§15), so Vestry controls it rather than inheriting it. Retention policy (how long per-attempt stores are kept, and what is exported to evidence before deletion) is the design question that remains. Token-side, the measured floor is ~20–24K input tokens per call regardless of task size, with prompt caching engaging across steps in a session (§8) — which is the number any cost or throughput model must start from. Not measured, and still open: memory, cold start, and UI responsiveness under three streaming workers.
11. **Readiness-certification schema and evaluation (D27).** The thirteen checks in §6 phase 3→4 are stated in prose because that is what a baseline holds. Design must decide: how each check is evaluated — from durable state, from the project record (Trello, D42), or from the repository — which are mechanically decidable and which need an LLM judgement call; what a certification record contains and where it lives (SQLite, mirrored to the project record as a comment, D42); what counts as a "material change" that invalidates one, expressed as a concrete set of triggers rather than a judgement; how a certification is re-obtained cheaply after invalidation so an invalidated certification does not stall the loop; and what the Chief does when a check is undecidable — the answer must be "the issue stays unready", never "assume ready". This is the highest-leverage open item from the adoption, because it is the gate that keeps an autonomous loop from executing against a stale spec.
12. **Comment-protocol parsing and idempotency (D28).** The marker vocabulary is fixed; its wire form is not. Design must decide the exact serialisation (a structured body under a marker heading, or a fenced JSON block), how a marker is made idempotent so a post-crash retry cannot duplicate a `START` or a `CLOSEOUT`, how markers survive the project record's markdown normalisation — the pre-cutover observation was Linear stripping strikethrough in this project's own D20-era evidence, and Trello's normalisation must be probed under D42 — what happens when a marker is malformed or written by something other than Vestry, and the ordering guarantee when several markers for one issue are in flight through the transactional outbox. Related: whether markers are parsed back from the project record (Trello, D42) at recovery time or only ever written, given that SQLite is authoritative and project-record content is untrusted input under D21.
13. **Vestry's own agent-entrypoint file.** A recursive consequence of the design that has no v1 analogue: **Vestry's workers are its first users.** The loop dispatches OpenCode processes into a target repository, and OpenCode reads that repository's agent instructions — `AGENTS.md` and `opencode.json` — as part of the worker's input. So the repository Vestry works in must ship instructions written for the agents Vestry will dispatch into it, and those instructions are simultaneously a feature of the product, a test fixture, and an R8 injection surface the product itself controls. Daemon Reborn's milestone ordering puts governance, a clean repository and an agent entrypoint **before** features, for the same reason. Design must decide: what that file contains, how it is kept honest against the baseline (D31 applies to it — it may not relax a requirement to make a worker behave), how it interacts with `OPENCODE_DISABLE_PROJECT_CONFIG` (§15), which removes project config but not `AGENTS.md`-style instruction files, and whether Vestry generates it into *other* repositories it works on or only maintains its own. **Note the unresolved tension:** §15 requires `OPENCODE_DISABLE_PROJECT_CONFIG` on every dispatch precisely because repository-local config is untrusted, while this item requires Vestry to author repository-local instructions its workers should obey. Both are right; the resolution is a design question, not a requirements question.

   **Partially resolved at revision 1.2.** D35 and §21 establish that `AGENTS.md` is a committed artefact of *this* repository alongside the baseline, so the "does the project ship one at all" half of the question is closed and the first such file now exists. What remains open: whether Vestry also *generates* one into repositories it drives, how it is kept honest against the baseline, and how it coexists with `OPENCODE_DISABLE_PROJECT_CONFIG` — since a project-instruction file the product wants honoured and a project-config file the product must neutralise are read from the same directory by the same child process.
14. **Goal-anchor and traceability schema (D33).** Design must decide: the anchor record's shape and what exactly is hashed (the criterion *text*, its executable check, or both — a criterion whose check was rewritten is arguably a new criterion, which is D31's boundary seen from the other side); how criterion identifiers stay stable across a charter amendment so the diff between anchor versions is computable rather than merely visible; where traceability edges are authored — the Planner writes them, or they are inferred from issue text, which would make drift detection depend on an LLM reading prose and defeat the point; the exact predicate for *orphan work* and *blind spots*, including whether a task that advances a criterion indirectly (a refactor enabling a later check) needs an explicit edge type or is noise; how "unresolved blind spot" at Phase 6 interacts with a criterion that is genuinely `NOT RUN` for a recorded reason; what the two-per-goal amendment cap is stored against when an Owner legitimately restates a goal mid-flight; and how the traceability graph renders usefully at fifty issues rather than the five a milestone-1 run produces.

## 19. First milestone — the proof that matters

**One real task, end to end, on a real repository, via OpenCode.** Not a fixture. The milestone is met only when all of the following are demonstrated and evidenced:

1. A goal enters through the UI and produces a charter with executable acceptance criteria.
2. The Chief plans it into at least one card on the bound Trello board (D42), with proofs attached on closure.
3. A Worker runs as an independent OpenCode process inside a leased worktree at a recorded base SHA.
4. **Cancellation is proven** — the worker and every descendant are demonstrably terminated, not merely acknowledged.
5. **Mid-run kill and restart reconciliation works** — the daemon is killed while a worker is live, and on restart the state is reconciled with no silent replay, no orphaned lease, and no lost user changes.
6. Verification, independent review, and bounded repair all execute, with the reviewer demonstrably not the author.
7. The evidence bundle reaches the attention inbox and the Owner accepts.
8. **The permission policy is proven in force, not merely set (D26).** A policy canary under a deny-all policy asserts the tool is absent from the model's tool list before any real work is dispatched, and a deliberately malformed `OPENCODE_PERMISSION` payload fails closed at the adapter rather than degrading silently to allow-all in the child.
9. **D20's load-bearing findings are re-proved on the models Vestry will actually run, under the never-paid policy (D44, revision 1.8).** The phase-2b probe ran on OpenRouter's free-model router, which rotates models and rate-limits, so default-allow enforcement, usage-on-stdout, orphan-on-hard-kill and `--session` resume reconciliation are re-evidenced against the pinned local open-weight model (weight digest recorded), and tool-calling parity is re-evidenced against one pinned free-tier model (provider, exact model identifier and served identity recorded); a run served by a different or rotated model is not a pass for the pinned model. The earlier "pinned paid model" wording is superseded and no paid model is used. The two recorded gaps — P2a cooperative abort and P6 live server endpoint probing — are either closed or explicitly re-recorded as capabilities the design does not depend on.
10. **The goal anchor holds, and drift is detected on purpose.** The anchor is frozen at Gate 1, every dispatched issue cites a criterion, and the two drift signals are demonstrated against deliberately injected faults rather than observed passing by default: an injected task that cites no criterion is **not dispatched** and surfaces as orphan work, and a criterion whose only covering work is withdrawn surfaces as a blind spot that raises an attention item. A charter amendment attempted for a third time in one goal **stops the loop** rather than drifting further.
11. **Tracking is real in both directions.** The goal produces the expected structure on the bound Trello board (D42) — cards in the workflow lists, the adopted taxonomy recorded on each card, its acceptance items as native checklists, and the D28 markers on every transition as card comments — and it is read back and verified there, not merely written. An empty or incomplete board at the end of a milestone-1 run is a failure of this item regardless of how good the local state looks, because the Owner's view of the project is that board.

Items 4 and 5 are the ones v1 never proved. They are the reason v2 exists, and the milestone is not met without them. Item 8 is the one the D20 probe turned from an assumption into a requirement: OpenCode's default is allow-everything, so a Vestry worker that reaches a dispatch without a proven policy is running with no envelope at all. Item 9 exists because the probe's evidence was honestly weaker than the conclusions it supported, and the milestone is the point at which they are earned at full strength. Items 10 and 11 were added at revision 1.2, and both were added **because they failed in practice before they were required**: the first milestone-1-shaped activity on this project produced zero Linear issues and a goal with no recorded traceability, which is exactly the failure mode D33 exists to make loud. Injected-fault demonstration is mandatory for item 10 for the same reason — a drift detector that has only ever been observed returning "no drift" has not been tested. At revision 1.8, D42 moves item 11's record from Linear to Trello and D44 re-scopes item 9 to the never-paid hybrid model policy; neither amendment relaxes an acceptance criterion (D31) — item 9 still requires every load-bearing finding at full strength, on the exact models Vestry will run, with served-identity recording retained.

## 20. Naming record

**Chosen name: Vestry.** Verified 6 November 2026.

A vestry is the body that administers an institution's temporal affairs and must report on what it did. That is the product: it runs the work in the Owner's absence and answers for the result.

### Criteria applied

1. **Semantic honesty** — points at accountable autonomy, not at "agent + noun".
2. **Sayable after one hearing**, and viable as a binary name, a crate name, and a folder.
3. **No confusing collision in this space.** This is the test that actually decides.
4. **Not AI-generic** — no `Agent-`, `Auto-`, `Copilot`, `Smart-`, `-GPT`.
5. **Ownable** — a free crate name, an obtainable GitHub organisation, an obtainable domain.

Exact-name-on-every-registry was tried first and rejected as a test: all four initial candidates were taken on crates.io, npm, PyPI and their primary GitHub handle. Single English dictionary words are saturated. Criteria 3 and 5 replaced it.

### Why Steward was rejected

Steward was the Owner's first choice and was rejected on substance, not availability:

- **crates.io `steward` is "Task runner and process manager for Rust"** — same language, same problem space, published since 2021, and the first search result anyone reaches.
- **Two live AI products own the name**, one of them positioned almost exactly against this product: Soleum's STEWARD, "the AI product expert for your digital product," built for regulated industries "where every answer has to be defended." Also getsteward.ai (AML compliance AI) and a Microsoft Store app, "Steward: Client & Project Manager."
- **"Open Source Software Steward" is a legal term of art under the EU Cyber Resilience Act** — a defined regulatory role with specific obligations. A developer-governance product named Steward would be permanently ambiguous in exactly the compliance conversations its evidence model belongs in. This is the most durable of the three problems.
- All GitHub handles were taken, including an organisation named "Steward" created 28 September 2026, and 46 crates match the search.

### Rejected this round on substance

`cartulary` (crates.io: "the knowledge layer of your project — decisions, issues, docs"; npm: a governance SDK for software agents), `viceroy` (Fastly's local testing daemon), `assay` (a widely used Rust testing macro, colliding with the verification angle), `ballast` (npm has quarantined the name against republishing; crates.io holds a load-testing tool), `reeve` (an allowlist-first policy runtime), `custos` (a GPU array engine), `oikos` (npm utility belt; consumer brand collisions), `bursar` (reads as finance-only), `fiducia` (abstract; reads as fintech).

## 21. Repository governance (D35)

The Owner created `https://github.com/Rohan0603/vestry` on 6 November 2026 and directed that Git and its conventions be used to maintain the project. This section is that directive turned into requirements. It applies to **this** repository, and every rule here is also a requirement the product must satisfy for the repositories it drives — the project is its own first customer, and a rule Vestry cannot satisfy on its own repository is a rule that will not survive contact with a real one.

**The repository is the versioned record.** The requirements baseline, the evidence files, the decision record and the agent entrypoint are committed artefacts. As of the state this section was written in, they were **not** committed — `main` held only `LICENSE` and `.gitattributes`, and the entire 110 KB baseline plus all four D20 evidence files were untracked working-directory files. That is a single-disk, single-copy failure waiting to happen and it is closed by the first commit under this section.

**Branching and merge.** `main` is the protected base and the only source of truth for accepted state. Work lands on `ves/<slug>` branches through a pull request (D42, revision 1.8; the older `ves/<issue-number>-<slug>` form remains valid where a legacy GitHub issue number is still meaningful). No direct push to `main`. No force-push and no branch deletion on `main`. No unattended auto-merge. A merge requires the review conditions in §6 phase 4 bound to the exact SHA (§7's Tier C is what makes this a boundary rather than a convention). Squash-merge is the default so one issue produces one commit; a rebase is allowed only on an unpushed branch.

**Commit convention.** Conventional Commits — `feat`, `fix`, `docs`, `test`, `refactor`, `perf`, `chore`, `ci`, `revert` — with a scope where one is meaningful, and the record card's Trello short link in the footer (D42, revision 1.8):

```
docs(requirements): add D42–D46 Trello-only record, self-merge and never-paid policy

Records the Trello-only system of record, the standing self-merge authorization,
the hybrid never-paid model re-scope, the deferred name actions and the gated
Projects cleanup authorization.

Refs: trello/<shortLink>
```

`<shortLink>` is the card's Trello short link — the eight-character token in `https://trello.com/c/<shortLink>/…` — which is stable across renames. Commits made before 13 November 2026 carry `Refs: VES-<n>` and remain valid history; they are not rewritten.

One logical change per commit. A commit that mixes a baseline edit with tooling is split.

**Traceability chain.** `trello/<shortLink>` card → branch → commit(s) → PR → merge SHA, each link recorded so any one resolves to the others (D42). The `Refs:` footer is what makes it mechanical rather than discoverable by luck. This is the same discipline §10 requires between the project record and SQLite, extended to the Git host.

**What never enters the repository.** `.env*` and any credential, SQLite database files, worker session stores, generated output (`target/`, `dist/`, embedded-asset build products), `node_modules/`, and runtime logs — enforced by a committed `.gitignore` reviewed as part of the change that adds a new artefact class. Evidence files are committed only after the §15 redaction pass, and the redaction is a recorded check, not a habit. A secret in a pushed commit is a Tier-C incident, not a cleanup task, because history rewrite on a shared host is not reliably reversible.

**Repository hygiene.** `README.md` is the entry point and states what the project is, what state it is in, and where the authoritative baseline lives. `AGENTS.md` is the agent entrypoint (§18 item 13). `CONTRIBUTING.md` carries the branch, commit and review rules above. `LICENSE` is the Owner's decision and stays in the reserved set (D34). Directory layout is decided by the agent and recorded, not asked.

**Release.** Tags are semver, signed where the toolchain allows, and a release exists only when its acceptance evidence exists. Packaging and installer concerns are §14's ("installation is copying an executable"), and the artefact is produced by CI from a tag rather than uploaded from a laptop.

**Branch protection — Owner action, not agent action.** Applying these settings on `main` requires repository administrator rights and is therefore in the reserved set: require a pull request before merging; require 1 approving review; dismiss stale approvals when new commits arrive (this is D30 enforced by the host rather than by good intentions); require status checks to pass; block force pushes; block deletions; require signed commits if the Owner's key is available. Until they are verified as *active*, every one of them is recorded as `target` and not `verified-enforced` (D32) — which is exactly the gap Daemon Reborn reports honestly against its own protection settings, and the reason D32 exists.

**Recorded reconciliation — D39 and D40 (revision 1.9).** The review-count and stale-approval wording above is the pre-D39 protection target, preserved as written. D39 replaced the one-approving-review requirement with an independent non-authoring-instance verdict bound to the exact head SHA — `required_approving_review_count` is 0 because the sole collaborator is the commit-author identity — and D40(c) reconciled the required-check set to `Policy validator tests` + `Gitleaks secret scan`. Stale-approval dismissal remains configured but is inert under D39, whose equivalent control is that any commit after the verdict invalidates the exact-SHA review record (D50). Where this paragraph and D39/D40 differ, D39/D40 govern, and any control named above that has not been demonstrated remains `target` (D32).

**Git is not a security boundary.** Filesystem and Git contents are source and artefacts; SQLite remains authoritative for runtime state, and no policy, approval or acceptance decision is read from a commit message as truth. A `Refs:` footer is traceability, not authorisation.
